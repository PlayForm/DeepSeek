## The challenge[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-challenge)
A model this size doesn’t run on one GPU. It runs on a node of eight H200s, and the question the challenge asks is **how fast can you make it go**? There are two tracks.
The **kernel track** takes the model apart into five pieces and asks you to make each one faster than [SGLang](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang), the inference engine Meta ships it with. Each piece is one problem, and each problem runs on a single H200:
  1. The dense projections (plain matrix multiplies).
  2. Attention during decoding.
  3. The routed experts, the part that holds 97% of the parameters.
  4. A whole decoder layer while decoding.
  5. A whole decoder layer during _prefill_ , when the model reads the prompt.


You submit a Python file. The judge runs it on an H200, checks that it gives the same answers as the reference, and times it against SGLang. A score of 1.00× means you tied; anything above means you won some time back. Kernel submissions close on November 4.
The **serving track** opens the day after, on November 5, and is where the money is: you patch SGLang itself and the judge runs the whole model on the whole node.
## Diving into Muse[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#diving-into-muse)
The problem statement for the prefill layer says “most of the time goes to the routed experts.” I copied SGLang’s baseline, ran the judge’s profiler, and it agreed: about 65% of the time is inside the experts.
And then I realized I couldn’t picture what an expert _does_ to a token. I could read the formula in the spec. I could see the shapes of the weight matrices. But I had no mental image of a token entering the layer, getting routed, being multiplied by a 4-bit matrix and coming out the other side. Making that faster without understanding it felt like running blindfloded.
So, this seemed like a great excuse to get to understand the model, piece by piece.

### Attention, then a gate[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#attention-then-a-gate)
The normalized goes through attention and comes back as , also [T,8192. In a plain transformer the next step is . Muse does something slightly different:
There are two differences. First, gets normalized before being added, so the attention output always has the same size no matter what came out of it. Second, instead of a plain sum we have a weighted sum: says how much of the old we keep and how much of the new information we add. Both are learned, and there is one value per dimension, so each of the 8,192 dimensions has its own mix. Meta calls this a learned residual gate. The reason is that with a plain sum gets bigger at every layer, and after 62 layers it can grow a lot. With the gate it stays in a fixed range.

### What differs between the 62[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#what-differs-between-the-62)

### 128 query heads, 16 key and value heads[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#128-query-heads-16-key-and-value-heads)
Having 128 query heads but only 16 key and value heads is called grouped-query attention: every 8 query heads share the same and . The reason is memory. The keys and values of every past token have to be stored (that is the KV cache), and that cost depends on the number of and heads, not heads. With 16 instead of 128, the cache is 8 times smaller. Muse also stores it in FP8, one byte per number. More on GQA in [the paper that introduced it](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13245).

### Sliding and full layers[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#sliding-and-full-layers)
The mask is causal in every layer, a token only sees the ones before it. In the sliding layers it also cuts everything further back than 2,048 tokens. That is the only difference between a sliding and a full layer.

RoPE, the rotation that tells attention how far apart two tokens are, is only applied in the 46 sliding layers. In the 16 full layers it is not applied at all, so those layers have no idea of position, only of content. ### The output gate[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-output-gate)
And finally . It has the same shape as the attention output, 128 heads of 64. The output of each head gets normalized and then multiplied, number by number, by , a sigmoid, so a value between 0 and 1. ### The KV cache at a million tokens[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-kv-cache-at-a-million-tokens)
Every token that goes through attention leaves its and in the KV cache of every layer, so that the tokens after it can read them without computing them again. Muse can read up to a million tokens, so this cache is the biggest memory cost of running it.
KV cachekvt1t2t3t4t5t6t6 writes its k and vt7the next token adds a rowq of t6is compared with every k in the cache
The KV cache of one layer and one key/value head: a row of k and a row of v for every token so far. The new token writes its row at the bottom, its q is compared with every k, and the next token will add one more row.
Done the plain way, with one key and value head for each of the 128 query heads, 64 numbers each in BF16, the cache takes  bytes, almost 2 MB per token. At a million tokens that is about 2 TB, more than the memory of all 8 GPUs together. Muse makes it about 60 times smaller with choices we have already seen:
  * Grouped-query attention: 16 key and value heads instead of 128, 8 times smaller.
  * FP8: one byte per number instead of two, 2 times smaller.
  * Sliding layers: 46 of the 62 layers only keep the last 2,048 tokens, so only the 16 full layers grow with the conversation, almost 4 times smaller.
  * The 8 GPUs split the 16 heads between them, 2 each, so each GPU holds an eighth.


In the end it is about 33 KB per token, about 35 GB at a million tokens, and about 4 GB on each GPU, next to the 141 GB an H200 has.

## Inside the Mixture of Experts[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#inside-the-mixture-of-experts)
s the residual stream RMSNorm h attention a RMSNorm mixture of experts to the next layer
The second half of the layer: the normalized stream goes into the mixture of experts, and what comes out, f, is mixed back into the stream by the second gate.
The second half of every layer is the mixture of experts. In a dense model this half would be one big feed-forward network, and every token would be multiplied by all of its weights. A mixture of experts splits it into many small networks, the experts, and each token only goes through a few of them. That is how Muse has 816B parameters but only uses about 42B for each token, and why this half is where most of Muse is: 799B of its 816B parameters, and in the challenge about two thirds of the time of a layer.

### The router reads the whole token, in FP32[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-router-reads-the-whole-token-in-fp32)
The router decides which 8 experts each token goes to, and it is just one matrix multiplication:

### 256 experts, 8 per token, no shared expert[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#256-experts-8-per-token-no-shared-expert)
Each layer has 256 experts, and every token goes through 8 of them. Different tokens pick different experts, so all 256 get used, but each token only pays for 8. Many recent MoE models also have a shared expert that every token goes through: [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) has one next to its 256 routed experts, and [Llama 4](https://ai.meta.com/blog/llama-4-multimodal-intelligence/) has one next to 16 or 128. Muse has none, so everything a token gets from this half of the layer comes from the 8 experts it picked. Having many small experts instead of a few big ones comes from [DeepSeekMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066): there are about ways to pick 8 out of 256, against 28 ways to pick the 2 out of 8 of Mixtral, so each token gets a much more specific mix.

### Inside one expert[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#inside-one-expert)
Each of the 8 experts is a small gated feed-forward network, the kind most recent models use, with three matrices of : gate, up and down. Gate and up both read , the token after the squeeze. The result of gate goes through an activation function and multiplies the result of up number by number, and down takes that back to 4,096 numbers. In a dense model up takes the token to a bigger size, about 3 times the model’s width, which is where the name comes from. Here the middle is also 4,096 wide, so the three matrices are square and up doesn’t actually go up.
What is different is what comes after: the expert normalizes its output. Most MoE models don’t have this norm. It puts the 8 outputs on the same size, so how much each one counts is decided only by the router’s weight. That output, times the router’s weight for this expert, is what gets added up with the other 7.

The original transformer used a plain feed-forward network, (x, with two matrices. ReLU turns negatives into 0 and leaves positives as they are. There, a unit has only one row, so the same number decides whether the unit fires and how much it writes, and it can never write a negative amount. The gated version splits that in two: the gate row decides whether, and the up row decides how much and with what sign. That costs one more matrix, and it trains better.

With the fader closed (left half) nothing is written, whatever the value; with it open, the value goes through with its sign (orange positive, blue negative).
So each unit is a rule of the shape “if the gate’s pattern is there, add this column, scaled by how much of the up feature there is”, and [this paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14913) shows feed-forward layers working like that. One expert has 4,096 of these rules, a layer has , and the whole model about 65M. A token only uses the rules of its 8 experts in each layer, which is what the router decides.
### Experts in a smaller space[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#experts-in-a-smaller-space)
In most MoE models every expert reads the token at the model’s width and gives back a vector of the same width. Muse first multiplies by a matrix that squeezes it from 8,192 down to 4,096 numbers, runs the experts at that size, and after mixing their outputs brings the result back to 8,192 with a second matrix. These two matrices are shared by all the experts. So each expert has three  matrices, about 50M parameters, and , the published number. At the model’s width every expert would be twice as big. What comes out of the second matrix is , the one the second gate of the layer mixes into the stream.

At 4,096 it needs three 4,096 × 4,096 matrices, and 256 × 62 of them are the 799B Meta publishes. The squeeze and the expand are paid once per layer.
Even at 799B the experts don’t fit: in BF16 they take 1.6 TB, more than the 1,128 GB of a node with 8 H200s. So Meta also publishes the weights in **NVFP4** , a 4-bit format where every 16 numbers share a small scale, about 4.5 bits per number, which brings the experts down to about 450 GB. [NVIDIA’s introduction to NVFP4](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvfp4-for-efficient-and-accurate-low-precision-inference/) explains how the format works. The H200 has no 4-bit math, so the weights are turned back into BF16 as they are read: the memory is saved, but the multiplications still happen in BF16.

## After the 62nd layer[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#after-the-62nd-layer)
After the 62nd layer, only the last token matters. Its stream goes through the output layer, a matrix that turns its 8,192 numbers into one score for each of the 202,048 tokens of the vocabulary, and one of them is picked, like the “in” at the end of the overview figure. That token goes back in at the start, as the next one. Everything the earlier tokens left behind is already in the KV cache of every layer, so for the next step only the new token has to go through the 62 layers.
## Running Muse on 8 GPUs[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#running-muse-on-8-gpus)
Even in NVFP4 the experts take about 450 GB, and one H200 has 141 GB, so Muse runs on all 8 GPUs of the node, and every layer is split across them. Attention is split by heads: each GPU takes 2 of the 16 key and value heads and the 16 query heads that go with them, so each GPU also keeps its own part of the KV cache. The experts are split whole: each GPU keeps 32 of the 256.
### 32 experts per GPU[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#32-experts-per-gpu)
The 8 experts of a token are usually on different GPUs. So the token is sent to every GPU that holds one of its experts, each of those GPUs runs it through that expert, and the outputs are sent back to be added up.

### Tokens travel in batches[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#tokens-travel-in-batches)
Doing this one token at a time would be far too slow, so Muse never does it. When it reads a prompt, all the new tokens go through each layer together, and while it generates, the next token of many conversations goes through together. That group is a **batch**. In the challenge, a turn of an agentic coding conversation brings 753 new tokens, so a batch there is 753 tokens.
All the tokens of a batch go through the router first, and each one gets its 8 experts. Then they are grouped by expert: every expert collects the tokens that picked it and multiplies all of them at once, in a single matrix multiplication. The reason is memory: an expert’s weights have to be read every time it runs, and this way they are read once for all its tokens instead of once per token, and the trips between GPUs carry many tokens at once. At the end each token gets back the outputs of its 8 experts and adds them up, weighted by the router. An expert that no token picked does nothing in that batch.

### When many tokens pick the same expert[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#when-many-tokens-pick-the-same-expert)
Those numbers are averages. The router picks experts by what each token is about, so some experts can end up much more popular than others. If many tokens of a batch pick experts that live on the same GPU, that GPU gets far more work than the rest, and the whole layer waits for it while the other seven are already done. That is why MoE models are trained to spread the tokens evenly, for example with the extra loss term that [Switch Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961) adds, which penalizes the router when it sends too many tokens to the same experts.

### Why the experts take most of the time[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#why-the-experts-take-most-of-the-time)
In the challenge the experts take about two thirds of the time of a layer. The first reason is that they have most of the math. In every layer a token is multiplied by about 690M weights, and 403M of them are in its 8 experts.

The weights one token is multiplied by in one layer, drawn to scale. Attention's two matrices are about a third; the 8 experts the token picked are more than half.
The second reason is less obvious. To run an expert, the GPU has to do two things: read the expert’s weights from memory, and do the multiplications with them. An H200 reads its memory, the HBM, at about 4.8 TB/s, and does about 989 trillion operations per second in BF16. The 4-bit weights of one expert are about 28 MB, so reading them takes 28/4.8≈ µs, and it takes almost the same whether the expert got 1 token or 500. The math is different: each token is multiplied by the expert’s 50M weights, a multiplication and an addition for each, so 100M operations, and 100/989≈ µs per token. A good kernel does both at the same time, so the expert takes as long as the slower of the two.

In a turn each expert gets about 24 tokens, so the math is done in 2.4 µs and the GPU spends the rest of the 5.9 µs waiting for the weights. Both take the same time at about 58 tokens per expert. Above that the math decides, which is the case when four conversations come at once (about 94 tokens per expert) and in the first turn, which reads the prompt in chunks of 16,384 tokens (512 per expert). This is also why the 4-bit weights matter for speed and not only for memory: in BF16, reading one expert would take about 21 µs instead of 5.9.

## Back to the five problems[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#back-to-the-five-problems)
With the whole model in view, the five problems of the kernel track are five cuts of the same layer. Each one runs on a single GPU of the eight, so it only gets that GPU’s part of the layer: 16 of the 128 query heads, 2 of the 16 key and value heads, and 32 of the 256 experts.
Four of them time Muse while it generates. Each step brings one new token for each conversation, with 1, 16 or 32 conversations at once and about 80K tokens of context each. SGLang’s default setup also uses [speculative decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192): each conversation brings a draft of 8 guessed tokens, and Muse checks all 8 in one step, so a step can also carry 8, 128 or 256 tokens. Those six batches are what the four problems are timed with. The fifth problem is the one from the experts section: reading a turn of 753 tokens, four conversations at once, and the first turn in chunks of 16,384.
The pictures are the ones on the [challenge’s site](https://openkernelchallenge.dev/#problems). Under each one, the two bars are SGLang’s time and the best submission’s, as they were on October 28.
### Dense projections[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#dense-projections)
This problem is every plain matrix multiplication of the layer, the ones that aren’t attention or experts: , , the router, and the two matrices that squeeze the token to 4,096 and bring it back to 8,192. They are in BF16, the router in FP32. With at most 256 tokens in a step, each weight is used at most 256 times every time it is read, so for most batches the time is just the time to read the matrices. This GPU’s part of is 38 MB, and reading it at 4.8 TB/s takes about 8 µs, almost the same whether the step has 1 token or 128. SGLang takes 4.83 ms per step for all of them, and the best submission 2.95 ms.
The five matrices one GPU holds, drawn to scale: the input runs along the top of each one and the output down its right side. From left to right, Wqkvg (8,192 in, 2,304 out: this GPU's q, k, v and g), Wo (1,024 in, 8,192 out), the router (8,192 in, 256 scores out, so thin it is almost a line), the squeeze (8,192 to 4,096) and the expand (4,096 to 8,192).
### Attention while generating[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#attention-while-generating)
This problem starts at the output of and ends at the output gate: QK-norm, RoPE in the sliding layers, writing the new and into the FP8 cache, attention, its norm and the gate. With one new token per conversation there is almost no math to do. The time goes into reading the cache: at 80K tokens, a full layer reads about 20 MB of it for every conversation, and a sliding layer, which only looks at the last 2,048 tokens, about 40 times less. SGLang takes 3.77 ms per step, and the best submission 1.23 ms.
The new token is the blue square on the right, and the tokens before it are the ticks on the line. Each arc is one earlier token it is compared with. On top a full layer, which reaches back over the whole context; below a sliding layer, which only reaches the last 2,048 tokens.
### Routed experts[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#routed-experts)
This problem is the 32 experts of this GPU, between the squeeze and the expand: for each token and each of its picks that lives here, the gate, up and down matrices in NVFP4, the norm on the output, and the router’s weight before adding the 8 up. The routing is skewed so that this GPU is the busiest one, the case from [when many tokens pick the same expert](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#when-many-tokens-pick-the-same-expert). With one token, only a few of its 8 picks land on this GPU. With 256 tokens, the experts in use get about 16 tokens each on average, below the 58 where the math starts to matter, so for most of them the time is reading their 4-bit weights. The routing also gives some experts many more tokens than others: the most popular one can get over 100. SGLang takes 13.45 ms per step, the most of the three pieces, and the best submission 5.01 ms.

### The whole layer while generating[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-whole-layer-while-generating)
This problem is the three pieces above together, plus the norms and the two gates of the stream: one whole layer, from the stream that comes in to the stream that goes out. The communication between GPUs is left out, and each GPU acts as if the other seven had added nothing. SGLang takes 22.62 ms per step, close to the 22.05 ms of the three pieces added up, and the experts are more than half of it. The best submission takes 9.41 ms.

### The whole layer while reading a prompt[#](https://memory-bound.vercel.app/blog/muse-spark-open-architecture/#the-whole-layer-while-reading-a-prompt)
This problem is the same layer at the shapes of the experts section: a turn of 753 new tokens on an 80K-token conversation, four conversations at once, and the first turn in chunks of 16,384. Most of the time goes into the experts, as one large grouped multiplication over all of them. As in the roofline above, a single turn still waits on reading the weights, while four conversations at once and the first turn give the experts enough tokens for the math to matter. It has its own leaderboard, because reading a prompt is not part of a generation step. SGLang takes 387.4 ms for the whole set of cases, and the best submission 190.6 ms.
